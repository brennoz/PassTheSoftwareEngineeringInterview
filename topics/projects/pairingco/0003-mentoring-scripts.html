Mentoring Scripts

PairingCo Moments & phrases  ·  What to say when · Lesson 3 of 3

The two engineers are playing junior developers. The scripts below aren't lines to memorise verbatim — they're the shape of what a mentoring response sounds like in each recurring moment, versus the shape of a purely technical response that misses the brief.

Q1: A "junior" asks "why not just do X?" (a quicker, hackier fix than yours)
SAY "That would work for today — the tradeoff is it hardcodes <assumption>, so it breaks the moment <realistic scenario> happens. I'd rather spend the extra two minutes now. Does that tradeoff make sense, or do you think that scenario's unlikely enough it's not worth it?"
WHY THIS SHAPE
Validates the suggestion first (it's not wrong, it's a tradeoff), states the concrete cost, then hands the decision back — that's mentoring, not overruling. A purely technical candidate just says "no, do it properly" and moves on, which reads as poor communication even if the code ends up the same.
Q2: A "junior" pushes back on your chosen pattern/approach
SAY "Good pushback. I went with <pattern> because <reason tied to the code in front of us>. Walk me through what you had in mind — if it handles <the same edge case> as well, I'm open to it."
WHY THIS SHAPE
Treats disagreement as signal, not a threat to defend against. Interviewers are explicitly testing whether you can be pushed off your first idea without getting defensive — this is the single most common "trap" in a pairing round.
Q3: You don't know a codebase convention or domain term
SAY "I don't know this codebase's convention for <thing> — how do you two usually name/structure this? That'll shape what I call it rather than me guessing and getting it wrong."
WHY THIS SHAPE
Reading unfamiliar code is graded explicitly. Guessing confidently and being wrong reads worse than asking — and asking model's exactly the behaviour you'd want a senior to model for a real junior on day one.
Q4: Time is running out and the refactor isn't finished
SAY "We're close to time — here's where I'd stop: <current state, tests green>. I haven't touched <remaining smell> yet; if I had another 20 minutes I'd tackle it by <one-line plan>, but I'd rather leave this in a safe, tested state than rush an unfinished change."
WHY THIS SHAPE
Directly matches "finishing is not the goal." Stopping cleanly with a stated plan for the rest scores better than a rushed, unverified last-minute change.
Q5: Naming the pattern you're applying
SAY "I'm pulling this into a <Strategy/Factory/etc> — the reason is <the concrete repeated branch/duplication in front of us>. I considered <alternative> but rejected it because <concrete reason>."
WHY THIS SHAPE
Naming a pattern without the "why here" and "what I rejected" clauses is just vocabulary. The rubric rewards the reasoning, not the terminology.
Q6: A "junior" offers a suggestion mid-change — do you take it?
SAY "Let's try that — quick way to find out is to run the test after. If it goes red we'll know why and back it out."
WHY THIS SHAPE
Treats their idea as a testable hypothesis instead of silently overriding or silently complying. Reinforces the safety-net habit from Lesson 2 at the same time.
