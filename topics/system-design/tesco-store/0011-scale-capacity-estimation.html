Scale & Capacity Estimation

Tesco Interview Prep · Tier 2 — Worked Example · File 11 of 12

File 0001's time budget puts capacity estimation right after requirements-gathering, before the diagram — the numbers here justify design decisions made throughout this folder rather than being decorative. Every figure below is a stated, round assumption for practicing the ESTIMATION SKILL, not a claim about Tesco's real numbers — say so explicitly if you do this live: "I don't know Tesco's actual store count, I'll assume roughly 3,000 for this exercise."

1. Base assumptions (state these out loud, then move on)

QuantityAssumed valueReasoning
Stores nationally~3,000Round number for a large UK supermarket chain across all formats (Superstore/Extra/Express/Metro)
Terminals per store (avg, across all formats)~20Small Express stores might have 2–4; large Extra/Superstore might have 30–40 staffed + self-checkout combined — 20 is a blended average, not a per-format number
Transactions per store per day~3,000A busy supermarket doing steady trade across opening hours, higher on weekends — round mid-estimate
Peak-to-average traffic ratio~5×Retail trading is spiky (lunchtime, after-work, weekends, Christmas) — 5× is a reasonable standard multiplier to assume without store-specific data

2. Derived numbers

WebSocket connections per store (file 0005's "not the big-scale problem" claim, checked):
  ~20 terminals × ~80% active at peak  ≈ 16 concurrent WS connections per store
  → trivially small for one local POS pod. Confirms the design assumption.

National daily transactions:
  3,000 stores × 3,000 tx/day  = 9,000,000 tx/day  (~9M)

National average tx/sec:
  9,000,000 / 86,400 seconds  ≈ 104 tx/sec  (nationally, hitting CENTRAL sync ingestion)

National peak tx/sec:
  104 × 5 (peak multiplier)  ≈ 520 tx/sec  (nationally, at peak)

Per-store tx/sec at peak (sanity check — this never leaves the store in real time):
  3,000 tx/day × 5 peak-multiplier / 86,400s  ≈ 0.17 tx/sec per store
  → confirms a single store's local write load is trivially small; ALL the
    interesting scale lives in the aggregate central ingestion path, not
    any single store's edge cluster.

Central sync payload volume at peak:
  520 tx/sec × ~2KB per sale event (items, prices, payment token, no PAN)
  ≈ ~1MB/sec ingress at peak
  → well within a single Azure Service Bus namespace's throughput; no
    unusual scaling problem here with standard managed-service limits.

3. What these numbers actually justify

NumberDesign decision it supports
~16 concurrent WS connections/storeFile 0005's claim that WebSocket connection-scaling isn't the hard problem here — confirmed by the maths, not just asserted
~0.17 tx/sec per storeFile 0004's edge hardware sizing (a modest small-form-factor box) — the local write load is nowhere near needing serious local compute
~1MB/sec central ingress at peakFile 0007's transport choice (Azure Service Bus, not needing Event Hubs-scale throughput) — the volume genuinely doesn't demand it
520 tx/sec national peak vs 104 averageFile 0008's autoscaling story (HPA needs to handle a real 5× swing, not a flat load) and the DR/multi-AZ argument (central genuinely does carry aggregate national load, unlike any single store)
Why doing this out loud matters more than the exact numbers The interviewer isn't checking your estimate against a real number they know and you don't — they're checking whether you can turn a vague requirement into a number, and then use that number to justify a concrete decision (Service Bus vs Event Hubs, edge hardware size, autoscaling targets) rather than picking components by vibes. Getting the assumed store count "wrong" doesn't matter; skipping this step entirely, or doing the maths without ever connecting it back to a design decision, does.
