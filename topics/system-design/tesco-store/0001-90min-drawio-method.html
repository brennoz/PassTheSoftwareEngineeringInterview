The 90-Minute Draw.io System Design Method

Tesco Interview Prep · Tier 1 — Transferable Method · File 1 of 12
What Tesco actually said (verbatim, from the interview invite) "Interview [round]: System Design. Duration: 90 mins. You'll be given a problem statement and will be asked to build an overview of a possible solution, using Draw.io. Once you have shown and explained your overview, you may be asked to go into more detail and provide some examples of CI/CD. Please feel free to ask lots of questions before trying to solve the problem. Throughout your interview, we'll ask you about your solution and the steps you're taking. We recommend having draw.io ready on your device before the interview begins."

Three facts fall out of that paragraph, and they should shape everything about how you prepare:

  1. The problem statement is unknown. It might not be about a Tesco store at all. Practicing one worked example (this folder's Tesco-store case study, files 0003–0012) builds fluency, but the actual skill being tested is the method in this file — applicable to whatever you're actually given.
  2. The deliverable is a live diagram, not prose. You will be watched drawing boxes and arrows in draw.io while thinking out loud. Fumbling the tool costs you time you don't have; not having a mental shape vocabulary costs you more.
  3. "Ask lots of questions" is explicit, graded guidance, not politeness. Jumping straight to a diagram without clarifying requirements is a known failure mode this prompt is pre-warning you against.

1. The 90-minute time budget

Nobody hands you a stopwatch in the room, so you have to bring your own internal one. The single biggest risk in a 90-minute live design round isn't a bad diagram — it's running out of time before you've said anything about trade-offs, failure modes, or CI/CD, because you spent 40 minutes perfecting box placement.

0–10 min Requirements clarification — ask questions BEFORE drawing anything (Section 2)
10–15 min Scale/capacity estimation out loud — turns vague requirements into concrete numbers that justify design choices later
15–40 min High-level design — the first full draw.io pass: every major box, every arrow, narrated as you draw
40–70 min Deep dive — interviewer picks 1–2 components and asks "go deeper here"; this is unscripted, driven by their questions
70–85 min CI/CD follow-up — guaranteed per the prompt; have a template ready (file 0002) rather than improvising
85–90 min Wrap-up — summarise trade-offs made, name what you'd do differently with more time
Why this isn't rigid You will not control this clock — the interviewer drives phase 4 (deep dive) by what they ask about. The point of memorising this budget isn't to enforce it minute-by-minute; it's so that if you notice you're 50 minutes in and still on the high-level diagram, you know you're behind and can say so out loud ("let me tighten this up so we have time for CI/CD") rather than silently running out of runway. Noticing and naming a time problem is itself a signal of seniority.

2. The clarifying-question framework

"Ask lots of questions" is the single most actionable sentence in the prompt. Most candidates ask two or three questions and start drawing. A strong answer works through four categories systematically — not because the interviewer expects you to recite categories, but because each category changes the diagram in a real, defensible way.

CategoryWhat you're really askingExample questions
Functional scope What must the system actually DO — and, just as important, what's explicitly out of scope? "Are we designing just the in-store checkout flow, or also inventory and pricing sync back to a central system?" "Do returns/refunds need to be in scope?"
Scale & non-functional What load, latency, and availability numbers actually drive the design? "Roughly how many stores / terminals per store are we designing for?" "What's an acceptable checkout latency — sub-second? A few seconds?" "Does the system need to keep working if the store loses internet?"
Constraints & existing systems What are you NOT free to redesign — existing infra, compliance, budget, team size? "Is this greenfield, or do we need to integrate with an existing central inventory/pricing system?" "Any compliance constraints I should design around — PCI-DSS for card payments, for instance?"
Edge cases & failure tolerance What happens when things go wrong — and how much does that matter for THIS system? "If a single till/terminal fails mid-transaction, what's acceptable — does the whole store stop, or just that till?" "Is eventual consistency acceptable for stock counts, or does it need to be exact in real time?"
Why this earns credit even when the answers are vague Interviewers often answer clarifying questions with "use your judgement" — that's fine, and expected. The credit isn't for extracting a precise spec; it's for demonstrating you know which axes actually change a design (scope, scale, constraints, failure tolerance) before committing pen to paper. State the assumption you're going with out loud ("OK — I'll assume ~50 terminals per store and design for the store staying operational if the WAN link drops, since that's the kind of thing a retail cashier system can't afford to get wrong") and move on. A stated assumption is much stronger than either silent guessing or interrogating for ten minutes without ever starting to design.

3. Draw.io shape vocabulary

You will not have time to hunt through draw.io's shape library live. Decide your vocabulary in advance and stick to it — consistency reads as competence even more than shape choice does.

ElementShape conventionWhen to use it
Client / actorStick figure or rounded rectangle, top-leftEnd user, cashier, external caller — where the flow starts
Load balancerSmall trapezoid or hexagonIn front of anything horizontally scaled
API Gateway / edge routerRounded rectangle, distinct fill colourSingle entry point fronting multiple services — also where you'd note auth/rate-limiting
Service (stateless, synchronous)Plain rectangleA Spring Boot microservice or equivalent, one box per bounded context, not per class
DatastoreCylinderLabel WHICH kind — "PostgreSQL", "Redis", "Cosmos DB" — a generic "DB" cylinder invites a "why this one?" you're not ready for
CacheCylinder, different colour, usually beside its serviceWhenever you'd actually put one — don't draw it just to look thorough
Queue / message brokerParallel lines / "pipe" shapeAny async boundary — this is also your visual cue to yourself to explain WHY it's async here
External system / third partyRectangle with dashed borderPayment gateway, external supplier feed, anything outside your control
CDN / edge cacheCloud shapeStatic assets, or geographically distributed read paths
Async / event arrowDashed arrowDistinguish from solid arrows (synchronous request/response) — this single visual distinction communicates a LOT about your design without extra narration
Colour discipline Pick 3–4 colours max and assign them to a meaning you state once early (e.g. blue = your new services, grey = existing/external systems, amber = data stores). Re-use them consistently. A diagram that develops its own colour logic as you go looks improvised; one with a colour key you state up front looks planned even if you're building it live.

4. The generic starting skeleton

Regardless of the actual prompt, most system design answers start from the same shape. Draw this first, then specialise it — it's faster than staring at a blank canvas trying to invent structure from nothing.

Client(s) │ ▼ [Load Balancer / API Gateway] ← auth, rate limiting live here │ ├──► [Service A] ──► [Cache] ──► [Datastore A] │ ├──► [Service B] ──► [Datastore B] │ │ │ ▼ (async) │ [Queue] ──► [Worker / downstream service] │ └──► [Service C] ──► [External system, dashed border] Cross-cutting (usually drawn as a small side box, not wired to everything): [Observability: logs/metrics/traces] [CI/CD pipeline → deploys all the above]

The specialisation work is: how many services, what splits them (bounded contexts, not arbitrary layers), which edges are synchronous vs async, and which store is SQL vs NoSQL vs cache — and being able to justify each of those four choices out loud is most of the actual interview.

5. What "good" looks like vs what fails

Strong signalWeak signal
Asks clarifying questions before drawing, across multiple categories from Section 2Starts drawing within the first minute
States assumptions explicitly when the interviewer won't pin down a requirementSilently guesses, or gets stuck asking for a precise spec that won't come
Names WHY a component exists ("cache here because reads outnumber writes ~50:1") before drawing itDraws a cache/queue/CDN because "systems like this usually have one," can't justify it when asked
Distinguishes sync vs async edges and explains the trade-offEvery arrow looks the same; no articulated reason for the boundary
Volunteers a failure mode unprompted ("if this service goes down, here's the blast radius")Only discusses failure modes if directly asked
Notices and names time pressure, adjusts paceRuns out of time still polishing the initial diagram
Has a ready CI/CD answer (file 0002) rather than improvising one liveVisibly caught off guard when CI/CD comes up, despite it being flagged in the prompt
The single most common failure mode Treating "build an overview of a possible solution" as "build THE correct solution." There is no single correct answer graded against a hidden key — the grading signal is how you think: the questions you ask, the trade-offs you name, the way you adapt when the interviewer redirects you into a deep-dive you didn't plan for. A merely-OK diagram with excellent narrated reasoning beats a beautiful diagram delivered in silence.
